01 / THE BIG INQUIRY · 0–12 MIN

More possibilities.
For whom?

Industrial Transformation

If people could produce more, travel faster, and live longer, what determined who actually benefited?

I can explain what the Industrial Revolution was, how it expanded human possibilities, and investigate what determined who benefited.

What changed?

The Industrial Revolution was a long transformation in how people produced goods: machines, new energy sources, and factories reorganized work. It began in Britain in the eighteenth century and spread unevenly. In this lesson, we focus on the United States, especially 1865–1920.

Machines increased what workers could produce. Steam engines made coal a source of mechanical power. Factory owners organized workers and machines in one production system. These changes expanded capability and created new conflicts over work, wealth, and the environment.

Quick partner check

One partner explains how a machine changes production; the other explains why the way work is organized matters. Then switch.

WATCH WITH YOUR CLASS · ABOUT 5 MIN

How inventions change history

The cotton gin is an earlier example, patented in 1794 after its development in 1793. It separated seeds from cotton; it did not pick cotton. Greater processing capacity increased demand for cotton and helped expand slavery. Watch for the connection between an invention and the institutions around it.

SURVIVAL

More chances to live

Life expectancy at birth summarizes survival under a year's death rates. High infant mortality pulled the average down; a value of 47 years does not mean most adults died at 47.

Life expectancy: 47.3 years in 1900 (death-registration states), 76.8 in 2000 (United States). Infant deaths: about 100 per 1,000 live births around 1900, 6.89 in 2000. Early coverage and estimation differ from later national statistics. Sources: CDC/NCHS.

04 / WHO BENEFITS? · 37–44 MIN

A bigger economy can still leave a wider gap.

Absolute well-being

What can a person afford or do? Food, lighting, travel, and health may improve even if the gap with richer people grows.

Relative position

How does a person's income compare with others? A larger gap can exist alongside improvements in both groups' lives.

Security and participation

Can a person keep their job, obtain care, attend school, and influence decisions? More goods do not automatically create these protections.

Try the logic

This is a made-up example, not historical data. A lower-income household's income rises from 10 to 15 units; a richer household's rises from 100 to 200. Both gain income, but the ratio grows from 10:1 to about 13.3:1.

Historical global estimates put the average income of the top 10% at less than 20 times that of the bottom 50% in 1820, and about 40 times in 1910. This is a global comparison, not a U.S. series. It cannot tell us by itself whether the bottom half's absolute income rose or fell. Source: World Inequality Report 2022, Figure 2.2.

FICTIONAL SCENARIO / CIRCA 1900

A working household

For this comparison, imagine a family whose teenager works in a mill. Their choices depend on wages, work demands, school access, and local services. We have not assigned exact wages or assumed the home lacks every convenience.

FICTIONAL SCENARIO / TODAY

A lower-income household

Imagine a family with electricity, public-school access, and a phone with internet access. These capabilities do not eliminate rent pressure, insecure work, or barriers to medical care. Other households may lack reliable access.
